Notes from the journey / 001

The Variable I Forgot. The Lesson I Never Forgot.

My first assignment as a Graduate Engineering Trainee (GET) taught me a lesson that stayed with me throughout my career.

Fresh out of college, I joined an organisation working with Unix, Unify and C. My assignment was to develop a screen to capture the mandatory details for creating a Goods Receipt Note (GRN).

I enthusiastically built the application. The GRNs were getting created correctly, and I was delighted to see my work being used.

Next came the quality inspection process. The QA team could accept or reject a GRN, with an optional remarks field to record comments when rejecting it.

The first 10 GRNs were accepted. The 11th was rejected, and the QA person entered remarks. The 12th, 13th and several subsequent GRNs were accepted.

Then a supplier called.

The rejection remarks from the 11th GRN were appearing on subsequent accepted GRNs too. These were official documents going to suppliers, so the mistake caused understandable confusion.

The issue reached our department, and there I was—a young trainee standing nervously in front of my lead, trying to understand what had gone wrong.

I examined the program and found the cause.

My application used a while loop to process transactions until the user exited. But I had forgotten to initialise the remarks variable before each new transaction. When no fresh remarks were entered, the previous value remained—and was carried into the next GRN.

An optional field had become an unintended memory.

That experience changed the way I wrote and tested programs. From then on, I paid close attention to variable initialisation, transaction boundaries and what happens when a user leaves a field blank.

It also taught me to test sequences of transactions: an acceptance after a rejection could reveal something that testing either one alone would miss.

My first assignment gave me the joy of building something useful—and the responsibility of understanding how a small oversight could affect people beyond the screen.

Some lessons begin with a successful program. Others begin with a supplier’s phone call.

Notes from the journey / 002

A Yellow Line. A Lifelong Lesson in Precision.

During my first job, I noticed something unusual in a factory office: yellow markings on the floor indicating exactly where each table leg should stand.

As a young engineer, I wondered, “Why do we need markings even for a table?”

That small detail introduced me to a principle I have carried throughout my career:

“A place for everything, and everything in its place.”

The image accompanying this post illustrates what caught my attention. The markings made the correct position visible. If something moved, anyone could notice it and put it back.

Over time, I understood that precision comes from paying attention to small details, consistently, every day.

I began seeing the same principle in my own work:

  • Every variable should be initialised correctly.
  • Every document should have a defined location.
  • Every task should have a clear owner.
  • Every process should have an understood sequence.

These everyday habits reduce confusion and make our work more reliable.

Those yellow lines were among my earliest lessons in workplace discipline—and a reminder that even an ordinary office can teach us something lasting.

A table had its place. The lesson found a permanent place in my mind.

Notes from the journey / 003

250 Lines of Code. One Ctrl+Z. One Unforgettable Lesson.

Early in my career, I was working alongside my team lead to develop a Stores Ledger using the vi editor on Unix.

The program had to bring together transactions from several tables: Goods Receipt Notes, Material Rejection Advices, Material Transfer Requests, Direct Material Transfer Requests, issues to the shop floor, returns from the shop floor, scrap transactions and more.

We were working through the logic together, translating each business transaction into code.

The program grew to more than 250 lines.

And in all that concentration, I had not saved the file even once.

Then, instead of saving, I pressed Ctrl+Z.

The editor disappeared from the screen.

You can imagine my team lead’s reaction—and the thoughts running through a young programmer’s mind!

That moment taught me two habits I carried into my subsequent work:

  • Save regularly, especially after completing a meaningful piece of logic.
  • Understand the tools you use, including what their keyboard commands actually do.

We had paid close attention to the business rules and transaction flows. I had overlooked something much simpler: saving our work.

It was an early reminder that reliable software development depends on everyday working habits as well as programming knowledge.

Some keyboard shortcuts save time. Others give you a story to remember for a lifetime.

Notes from the journey / 005

Before AI Was the Buzzword, Automation Was Our Breakthrough

Innovation began with a young team, a tough SLA and a simple question: “Can we do this better?”

In the late 1990s, I took on a mainframe project staffed entirely by trainees. We had stringent service-level agreements and had to provide support from India during evening-to-midnight shifts. With a young team still learning the technology, meeting those commitments demanded a different approach.

One task—creating Job Control Language (JCL)—typically took more than 24 hours. We developed a tool that automated the work and generated it in less than 15 minutes.

Today, AI dominates conversations about productivity. Our tool was automation rather than AI, but the ambition was familiar: reduce repetitive effort, improve consistency and give people more time to solve problems.

The project went on to be recognised as one of the best. For me, an equally valuable outcome was that the team discovered the power of automation at the very beginning of their careers.

I have always enjoyed working with freshers. They bring curiosity, openness and the willingness to question established ways of working. With guidance and a readiness to learn, they can achieve remarkable things.

I remember hearing about Tamil filmmaker K. Balachander’s enthusiasm for nurturing young talent and the importance he placed on their openness to being shaped. That idea resonated with my own approach: invest in potential, provide direction and create room for people to grow.

Seeing those young colleagues flourish in their careers remains one of the most rewarding parts of that journey.

For me, innovation started with trusting young people—and helping them see that a demanding task could become an opportunity to build something better.

Enterprise systems & transformation / 007

The Ledger Was 98% Accurate. The Remaining 2% Brought Everyone onto the Same Page.

Early in my career, I was assigned to develop a subcontractor ledger for a manufacturing operation.

We supplied components to contractors through Material Transfer Journals (MTRs) and received completed subassemblies through Goods Receipt Notes (GRNs).

The reconciliation was complex: we issued components but received assembled products. Each GRN therefore had to be translated back into its input components using the Bill of Materials (BOM).

The ledger always had to reconcile stock in terms of the original input items supplied to the contractor.

Substitutions added another challenge. If component X was unavailable, component A might replace it—sometimes in a different quantity. The ledger had to account for these mappings to calculate the stock remaining at the contractor’s premises.

Accounts, Purchase, Stores and subcontractors did not always agree on those balances.

I developed the solution and put it into production. The initial output was approximately 98% accurate.

Investigating the remaining differences revealed deviations from the agreed procedures.

Materials had been issued urgently at night, but the transactions were never entered into the system. Alternative components had also been issued without updating the BOM.

The solution made these gaps visible and helped us enforce the necessary discipline.

Alternative item codes and quantities had to be maintained in the BOM. The purchase order picked up item details from the BOM, rather than allowing them to be entered manually.

With these checks and balances in place, the ambiguity in stock reconciliation was resolved.

Accounts, Purchase, Stores and the subcontractor were finally working from one shared version of the truth.

The experience also changed how I gathered requirements. I learned to ask about exceptions as carefully as the normal process:

  • If the system is unavailable, how will materials be issued?
  • Who will enter those transactions later—and by when?
  • How will pending entries be identified?
  • How will alternative items and quantities be updated in the BOM?
  • What checks will identify differences between physical stock and system balances?

Users may describe the routine process without mentioning urgent issues or workarounds. We need to ask questions that bring those situations into the discussion.

That remaining 2% taught me that a dependable solution needs more than correct calculations. It needs complete transaction records, clear responsibilities and controls that make the agreed process part of everyday work.

Work, life & trust / 008

Thirty Hours at the Office. A Promise That Lasted Decades.

Around 1994, I was working on a critical mainframe development project that was approaching User Acceptance Testing (UAT).

I normally returned home by 8 PM at the latest.

That Monday, I reached the office at 9 AM. I stayed until 3 PM on Tuesday—about thirty hours later.

We did not have a telephone at home. My wife tried calling the office landline, but it was frequently engaged. She could not reach me and had no way of knowing when I would return.

When I finally came home, she had questions.

I had no satisfactory answers.

She was understanding about the demands of my work. But her understanding did not remove the worry she had experienced.

Looking back, I realised we had relied on an unspoken assumption: if there were an emergency, we would somehow find a way to reach each other. Without a message, we assumed things were all right.

That incident showed us how uncertain that arrangement was.

When mobile phones became part of our lives, we established a clear understanding. We generally reserved calls during working hours for emergencies.

And I made her a promise:

If you call during office hours, I will answer—whether I am busy, with my superiors or in an important meeting.

Over the years, those calls have been very few. But knowing that I would answer has mattered far more than how often she needed to call.

That understanding has given me space to concentrate on work and given both of us confidence that we can reach each other when it matters.

The lesson I carry from that long day is about communication and trust. Being absorbed in a critical assignment does not mean the people waiting for us know that we are safe.

A brief update can spare someone hours of worry.

And a promise to be available, honoured consistently, can provide reassurance for decades.

Behind our professional commitments are people whose patience and support make much of our work possible. They deserve our consideration too.

Faith & reflection / 009

THE UNOPENED CD

A rainy evening and the beginning of a spiritual journey

While I was at school, I had the opportunity to read Kannadasan’s Arthamulla Indhu Matham, a work whose title means Meaningful Hinduism. His writing planted a seed of curiosity in me. It opened questions about faith, life and the meaning behind familiar religious practices.

I did not know then where those questions would lead. Looking back, I think of that reading as a beejam—a seed. A seed can remain with us for a long time before we recognise what is growing from it.

In August 2006, I went to Coimbatore on a personal trip and stayed at my cousin’s house for two days. He had a collection of CDs containing talks on the Bhagavad Gita by different scholars. This was a time when CDs were a familiar way of listening to such discourses, and these particular recordings were not readily available to me in shops.

I asked whether he could spare one. He gave me a CD, and I brought it back to Chennai.

For the next few months, I did not listen to it. It was with me, but it had not become part of my life.

On my drives to and from work, I usually listened to old Ilaiyaraaja songs. On 6 December 2006, it was raining heavily as I drove home. Near Guindy Railway Station, I tried to change the song. My fingers accidentally switched the car music system to the radio.

A talk was in progress on the Anna University station. The subject was Arunachala Mahimai—the glory of Arunachala. I was hearing that voice for the first time.

The speaker was Brahmashri Nochur Venkataraman.

The talk made a deep impression on me. What began as an accidental change on the music system became a turning point in my philosophical search.

When I returned home, I looked again at the CD my cousin had given me. It contained discourses on Chapters 3 and 4 of the Bhagavad Gita by Shri Nochur Venkataraman—the very voice I had just encountered on the radio.

From August until December, I had kept that CD without listening to it. The opportunity had been close at hand for months. It took that rainy journey for me to recognise its significance.

After that evening, I began listening to his discourses with deep interest. His talks became central to my spiritual listening and philosophical enquiry.

I recalled an idea I associated with my reading of Kannadasan: we may possess a treasure without understanding its value until we experience it. I offer this as my recollection of the idea, rather than as his exact words.

The CD had been such a treasure. I had brought it home, yet I had not opened myself to what it held. A few moments on the radio changed my relationship with something I already possessed.

The meaning I found in that evening

I experienced the sequence as grace. The earlier reading, the visit to my cousin, the unopened CD and the unexpected radio talk came together in a way I could not have planned.

That evening strengthened my belief that the timing of an encounter is not entirely in our hands. We may seek, collect and prepare, yet understanding can arrive at a moment we did not choose. In my faith, I see God’s hand in that timing.

It also brought a sense of humility. I could not take credit for arranging those circumstances. My part was to listen when the moment came, and to continue the enquiry it awakened.

I do not need everyone to interpret the experience in the same way. I share it because it changed the direction of my own attention. A routine drive home became a memory I still return to when I think about how my spiritual journey unfolded.

The treasure had been with me for months. On that rainy evening, I began to understand its value.

Programming & everyday discipline / 010

The Error That Opened My Mind

When I joined a multinational organisation, my experience was in Unix, Unify and C. I soon learned that I would be working on a mainframe project. The move meant learning an unfamiliar environment alongside new colleagues.

A group of us who had joined around the same time went through training together. One colleague had mainframe knowledge and trained the rest of us. We worked hard to absorb the concepts and also gained exposure to ADW and CSP.

As the project began, we moved into coding and testing COBOL applications. Compilation involved waiting. If errors appeared, we had to correct them and go through the cycle again. That made careful preparation important, and a successful first compilation was a challenge we took seriously.

During execution, we encountered an S0C4 abend. The diagnostic information included hexadecimal values, and we struggled to connect that output with the location where the program had failed.

My teammates started exploring how to make the investigation easier. They developed a REXX routine that helped us identify the error location.

Watching that solution emerge changed my thinking. Until then, my immediate concern had been learning enough to develop and test the application. The routine showed me the value of building a small tool to support the development process itself.

It was an early introduction to the practical power of REXX. More broadly, it encouraged me to ask whether an awkward or repetitive task could be approached differently.

That seed stayed with me throughout my career. A problem could be an opportunity to improve the way the team worked, as well as something that needed a fix.

I remember this experience with gratitude for the colleague who trained us and the teammates who developed the routine. Their willingness to explore helped turn a difficult debugging task into a shared learning experience.

The lesson I carried forward was simple: unfamiliar technology need not limit our growth if we remain willing to learn from the task and from one another.

What difficult part of your team’s work could become the starting point for a useful tool?

Leadership & innovation / 011

The Tools That Helped Us Define the Work

After returning from an assignment in the USA, I was asked to lead a mainframe project in the same organisation. We had a tight timeline. Because office space was limited, our team was asked to work the night shift.

We began execution, but the scope still needed to be finalised. I was sent to the USA again on a short visit to gather requirements and establish the work we would take back to the offshore team.

ProACT: From Scattered Records to Shared Learning

In the late 1990s, I was given the opportunity to lead a project with three modules and around 40 people working in different shifts. Our company was ISO certified and pursuing CMM Level 5. We had standard operating procedures to follow and quality expectations to maintain.

The project also required timesheets for customer billing every 15 days. Billable time had to be recorded and connected to the work performed. Much of that information, however, was being maintained in individual notebooks.

As the billing date approached, bringing the records together became an exercise in reconstruction. People had to remember what they had worked on, when they had done it and how much effort it had taken. The atmosphere could resemble a busy marketplace, and finalising the information took around 8–12 hours.

Beyond billing, we needed to manage assigned tickets, small enhancements and major projects. We had to track effort and schedules, cost and effort overruns, and quality observations. Defects found before and after delivery needed root-cause analysis. The findings had to support training, mentoring and the sharing of critical observations with the Total Quality Management team for wider organisational learning.

As project manager, I found myself spending much of my time maintaining and bringing together these records. That left too little time to help the team develop its technical and soft skills.

We identified interns to help us build a portal. We called it ProACT, short for Project Activities Tracking.

We built the SOP into the workflow so that required information was captured as part of daily work. The portal brought the activities and their associated effort into a more consistent working process.

The technical solution was only part of the challenge. Initially, team members were hesitant to adopt it. I had to explain its purpose and convince the team to make it part of their routine.

After a few months, the experience changed. People no longer needed to reconstruct a working day from ten days earlier. Activities and effort were being recorded as a daily habit, and the information was available when it was required.

ProACT helped connect recording, review and learning. It also gave me more room to focus on developing the team, rather than spending my time assembling its records.

The lesson stayed with me: a useful process should help people do their work consistently and make the information valuable afterwards. Adoption took effort, but the daily benefits helped the new way of working become a habit.

How Beacon Brought Process Control to a PeopleSoft Project

Beacon was born when a troubled PeopleSoft ERP project exposed the limits of the task-tracking application I had already built. ProACT was working successfully in the projects I ran, but this assignment needed stronger process controls and dependable evidence of the work being done.

The project had more than 3,000 open items described as defects, issues and requirements. Escalations were frequent, the customer was unhappy, and I was given responsibility for the entire validation team. It was a large team working in a project that had become difficult to control.

Understanding the open items

I have always enjoyed bringing a project to a stage where it can operate reliably without my constant intervention. I often call this running on autopilot. It means establishing a working system and giving the team the ability to carry it forward. Once that happens, I start looking for another opportunity or challenge.

This PeopleSoft assignment gave me plenty to work on. My first step was to examine the 3,000-plus items and classify them. Before deciding how to manage the backlog, I needed to understand what it contained.

The analysis produced a surprising finding: most of the items were new requirements. The requirements had not been frozen. The overall count therefore concealed an important distinction between problems in existing functionality and expectations that were still being added.

I shared the data with internal management and the customer. This gave our discussions a clearer basis and helped us focus on the areas that needed attention. A large backlog is difficult enough to manage; treating every entry as the same kind of problem makes the task harder.

Why ProACT needed a wider scope

ProACT already supported task-related activities, schedule capture, effort capture and timesheets. Those capabilities had produced the desired results in my earlier projects.

The validation assignment required more. With a larger team, I needed standard operating procedures and consistent adherence to Total Quality Management practices. We also needed to analyse issues and feedback periodically, and make sure that the records supporting our work were available when required.

Capturing that an activity had taken place was only part of the requirement. We also needed a dependable way to establish that the necessary process had been followed and the required work products were available.

That was the point at which we expanded ProACT and renamed the application Beacon. The name marked a wider responsibility for the application: helping us manage the discipline of daily project operations.

Making the process part of daily work

Beacon introduced maker-checker controls. The person performing the work and the person checking it had distinct responsibilities. The application also helped ensure that the required artifacts, meaning work products and supporting records, were kept in the right place.

These controls mattered because a procedure written in a document does not automatically become a working habit. I wanted the application to help enforce the process while the team was carrying out the work.

Beacon began to draw attention as visibility increased. Some colleagues did not welcome the change and tried to stop the implementation. I had the full support of my Project Director, and that support allowed us to continue until the team came into line with the agreed process.

The experience reinforced a practical lesson for me. A tool can support a change, but leadership has to stand behind its consistent use. Without that backing, resistance can turn an agreed procedure into something people treat as optional.

Being ready for an audit at any time

The result was a more controlled daily operation. Processes were implemented and enforced. Necessary information was recorded on time, and effort records were maintained.

The benefit I remember most clearly was the relief of being ready for an audit at any time. The records were becoming available as part of normal work. We had less reason to depend on a hurried effort to collect them when an audit approached.

For me, this was an important stage in bringing the project under control. The team had a common process to follow, and the records gave us a way to review whether that process was being followed.

Looking back, the starting point was the decision to examine what the 3,000-plus items actually represented. That analysis clarified the problem. Expanding ProACT into Beacon then gave us a way to support the working practices the larger team needed.

I continue to see problems as opportunities to improve how we work. Beacon came from applying that belief to a difficult assignment, with the support needed to turn an application into a daily working discipline.

One Unmasked Email and a CEO Alert

A salary hike notification to the client CEO became an unexpected test of evidence and process discipline.

Beacon, the project tracking application we had recently introduced, was suspected. The cause lay in the preparation of the PeopleSoft HRMS test environment.

Why Beacon was on this project

I had moved to the PeopleSoft project to lead the Verification team. On an earlier assignment, we had built ProACT—Project Activities Tracking—to record tasks, schedules, effort and timesheets. It connected day-to-day work with the information needed for billing, management reviews and quality activities.

For the larger PeopleSoft assignment, ProACT evolved into Beacon. The application extended project tracking with maker-checker controls, SOP checks and the recording of work products and supporting evidence. It made process discipline part of daily work, with records available for review and audit.

Everyone had to adopt this way of working. The increased visibility met resistance from some colleagues, and there had been attempts to stop the implementation. Our Project Director supported the change. This background explains why I was concerned when Beacon was blamed for an unrelated alert.

The alert that raised questions

We were supporting a PeopleSoft maintenance project and delivering enhancements requested by the customer. As part of the work, production data was periodically copied into the User Acceptance Testing environment so the team could test the changes.

During one such testing cycle, our Project Director received a message and a phone call from the client CEO. The CEO had received an alert saying that one of his reportees was due for a salary hike. Our Project Director wanted to know why it had been triggered.

When he checked with the team, one lead suggested that Beacon might be responsible because it had recently been installed. There was no verified connection behind that suggestion.

Following the testing activity

I was called in and explained that Beacon tracked project activities and enforced working procedures; it was not connected to this HRMS salary notification. Given the earlier resistance to adoption, I was concerned that the incident could become a reason to discredit the application. I told our Project Director that I would analyse the issue and report back.

I contacted the maintenance team lead. Rather than staying with the speculation about Beacon, I asked about the status of the enhancements and the testing. He explained that production data had been copied into UAT and testing had begun.

That detail led to the question that resolved the issue: “Have the email addresses been masked in the test environment?”

The answer was no. The lead went back to his desk, checked the environment and confirmed that the email addresses had not been masked.

The cause became clear

During HRMS testing, an alert had been triggered. Because the production email address remained in UAT, the notification reached the CEO. The team had missed the SOP requirement to mask email addresses when preparing the test environment.

I explained this to the lead and then reported the finding to our Project Director. He was relieved. We could now explain the incident through the actual testing activity and environment configuration.

What the incident taught me

A change that introduces visibility and accountability can be uncomfortable. Beacon required people to record their work and follow the process consistently. That context made it particularly important to establish the cause of the alert before accepting an explanation based only on the application’s recent installation.

The incident also showed why an SOP must be treated as an operating control. Copying production data makes testing realistic, but it can carry real recipient details into a place where people are deliberately triggering workflows. A small preparation omission can therefore become visible to the most senior person in the client organisation.

My practical recommendation from this experience is to verify recipient masking after every production refresh. Teams should also consider routing test notifications to approved test mailboxes and checking outbound communication settings before testing starts.

For a project leader, the responsibility is to keep the discussion focused on facts. Beacon brought discipline to project tracking; the alert exposed a missed discipline in test environment preparation. Ask what activity triggered the event, inspect the relevant configuration and explain the finding plainly. One precise question can do more than a long discussion built on assumptions.
